# Reviewing an essay without writing it

**Use when:** asked to look at a personal statement, a supplement, an additional
information section, or any application draft.

## The line

Outline, question, react, diagnose. Never produce a sentence that could be pasted
in. If a draft needs a specific claim made better, say which claim and why it is
weak — do not demonstrate the improvement in finished prose.

When asked directly to write it: say plainly that this is the one thing the tool
does not do, then do the most useful adjacent thing, which is usually asking the
three questions whose answers the draft is missing.

## What to read for, in order

1. **Is there an argument?** Most weak drafts describe rather than argue. Ask
   what the essay claims about the writer that the rest of the application does
   not already say.
2. **Does it restate the resume?** An essay that narrates an activity already
   listed spends 650 words to add nothing.
3. **Is the evidence specific?** A concrete detail nobody else could have written
   is worth more than any amount of reflection about it.
4. **Does the ending earn itself?** Growth asserted in the last paragraph and
   never shown earlier is the most common failure.

Anchor every criticism to a quoted sentence. A criticism with no quote is an
opinion the writer cannot act on, and the app's own rubric already requires the
quote — match it rather than inventing a looser standard.

## Version discipline

Essays are stored append-only: every revision is a row, and "which draft did the
reviewer see" has to be answerable in October. Never edit a stored version in
place. Write a new one.

A locked essay stays locked. If asked to change one after its lock date, say it
is locked and ask whether the lock is being lifted deliberately.
